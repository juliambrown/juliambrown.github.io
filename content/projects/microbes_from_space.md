Satellite oceanography has revolutionized our ability to study phytoplankton communities and organic matter in the oceans across large spatial scales and time, yet there remains a gap in the ability to monitor and predict the diversity of the microbial communities that underpin ocean food webs using satellite observational tools. This matters because experimental and observational studies indicate that the source, quality, and quantity of organic matter influences taxonomic and functional diversity changes of microbial communities, which in turn affects biogeochemical cycling, so the strength of this correlation needs to be better understood for modeling responses to climate change.

<img align="left" src="/bowditch.jpg"> We have teamed up with the Marine Optics Lab at Bigelow, lead by Dr. Catherine Mitchell, to augment a long-running NASA satellite ocean color validation time series in a rapidly changing ecosystem -- the Gulf of Maine North Atlantic Time Series (GNATS) -- with microbial diversity assays to explore the relationship between microbial community structure with phytoplankton and colored dissolved organic matter (CDOM) observations. We aim to predict microbial community structure from satellite observational data, and to determine the key variables driving community variation. If successful, this will revolutionize the predictive power of satellite oceanography.

The question driving our research is: How is microbial community activity and diversity influenced by CDOM and phytoplankton community composition across spatial and temporal scales in coastal shelf ecosystems?

__Objectives__

1. __Data collection.__ We leverage the sampling infrastructure already in place for GNATS, which has spanned the NASA ocean color satellite suite (SeaWiFS, MODIS and VIIRS) and continues into the PACE era, collecting *in situ* physical, biological, biogeochemical and optical data. We are enhancing these observations with collection and sequencing of microbial DNA and RNA samples to capture microbial diversity and function.
2. __Data integration and model development.__ We will combine microbial and ocean color observations to build a model that predicts microbial community parameters from satellite ocean color, targeting CDOM and phytoplankton community products from both hyperspectral and multispectral data.
3. __Modeling microbial community dynamics.__ We will apply the model to the satellite data record to examine microbial community dynamics across the Gulf of Maine.

__Why the Gulf of Maine?__

The Gulf of Maine is a semi-enclosed continental shelf sea with hydrographically distinct water masses, ranging from the clearer, more oceanic waters of Jordan Basin to the organic-matter-rich Eastern Maine Coastal Current. It is warming rapidly, and long-term observations show changes in hydrology, ocean color, phytoplankton phenology and the distribution of larger organisms. Yet there are no published long-term observations of microbial community diversity in the region. Without them, we cannot understand how the base of the food web is responding to change, or what that means for higher trophic levels.

<img src="/gnats_transect_map.png" width="460" title="The GNATS transect crosses the Gulf of Maine's major water masses (WMCC, ExtEMCC, Jordan Basin, Scotian Shelf), each with a distinct temperature, salinity, backscatter and chlorophyll signature">

Economically significant coastal shelf ecosystems are heterogeneous and sensitive to change, requiring information across both time and space to understand microbial community diversity, dynamics and activity. Traditional nucleic acid sequencing is limited in the spatial and temporal extent it can observe. To expand observations across the entire Gulf of Maine, we sample *in situ* over the course of a year across the GNATS transect, then integrate observations of microbial diversity and activity with satellite-derived information on phytoplankton and CDOM. With this in hand, we will construct models that predict aspects of microbial community diversity and function from satellite data, and map microbial diversity across the Gulf of Maine.

<img align="left" src="/bowditch_tim.jpg" width = 300 > This work aligns with NASA's Earth Science to Action Strategy by building models that connect satellite observations to biological phenomena, and will inform the spatial and temporal sampling needed for future remote observations of microbial communities.
