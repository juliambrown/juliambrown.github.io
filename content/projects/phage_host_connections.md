Viruses are the smallest but most abundant biological entities on the planet. Thanks to advances in low-cost DNA and RNA sequencing, we are now better equipped to detect and catalog the genomes of wild, uncultivated viruses. However, for most discovered viruses, we still don't know which hosts they infect, a major barrier to understanding their ecological roles. A central goal of our research is to bridge this gap: linking viral diversity to the microbial hosts they infect using cutting-edge sequencing technologies and bioinformatics tools.

__Single cells as anchor points__

Single cell genomics, the separation and sequencing of individual DNA-containing particles, yields not only the DNA content of individual cells but also any other DNA present in and on the cell at the time of capture. This allows an assessment not only of individual microbial genomes, but of the whole DNA landscape within that cellular particle.

In our research, we use **single amplified genomes (SAGs)** to identify viral DNA within randomly sampled cells, revealing virus-host linkages and other ecological interactions. To generate SAGs, cells are selected and separated by flow cytometry, and their genomes are amplified and sequenced individually. If a virus is infecting a sorted cell, its DNA is sequenced alongside the host DNA, directly linking the virus to its host. This matters because identifying the hosts of uncultivated environmental viruses is a **major challenge** in the field.

Using these single cell-based virus-microbe connections as anchor points, our research integrates, explores and compares other tools and data types, such as MetaHi-C and *in silico* methods for identifying the hosts of phage genomes, across a variety of aquatic marine systems. Comparing methods lets us confirm and strengthen observations made across approaches.

__Epigenetics at the virus-host interface__

One way we are doing this is by pairing single cell genomics data with Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) sequencing data to study epigenetic modification.

Epigenetic modification likely plays important roles in microbial physiology and defense against viral pathogens, and is likely an important strategy for both phage defense in bacterial hosts and host evasion in phages. Yet few studies to date have characterized the prevalence or patterns of epigenetic modification within marine microbes and their viruses.

This research aims to strengthen the use of ONT for methylation detection by leveraging paired datasets of SAGs and long-read metagenomic and metaviromic sequencing. We identify methylation rates and patterns across individually verified genomes, and compare the rates and patterns of methylation between viruses and their hosts.
